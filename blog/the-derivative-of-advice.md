# The derivative of advice

There’s a quote I think about often from John Ternus: “Always assume you're as smart as anyone else in the room, but never assume that you know as much as they do.” I used to underestimate my capabilities. I’ve grown since then, and have the confidence to step into the fog of war, knowing I can work it out. But that hasn’t eroded the other half of the quote. Others know far more than I do, but how do I discriminate useful vs useless information, and do it fast? Everyone has a take. Listening to them all is a poor use of resources. These are the guidelines I’ve come to follow over time.

## 1. Velocity outweighs position

This wasn’t initially clear to me. I’d grown up chasing prestige, and looked up to those who’d earned those merits. It’s when you garner those credentials that you realize the path to reach them is bullshit. Credentials measure position, where someone is right now. What matters is velocity, how fast they’re getting better at something real. More often than not, it’s those who focus on real problems, sharpen their skills towards a greater vision, that lead the world. The credentials, merits, and prestige follow. Don’t listen to someone purely because of their rank. That path is formulaic. People tend to take the plug and play route, and there’s not much that can be learned from the conventional path. Discern value from their ability to make hard turns. The decisions they made, the reasoning behind them, and the conviction that powered it all.

## 2. Inflection points matter

Inflection points occur when acceleration inverts. Linear growth gets you a steady path, but sharp inflections force you to compound wisdom. Wins and losses count towards that. The more inflection points in a set amount of time, the more they’ve experienced. Everyone knows how to walk a straight line; it’s when you’re thrown off track that your strengths shine. I’ve grown to become comfortable sharing my fair share of failures, because I realized it doesn’t convey weakness. It builds identity. You are who you are because of the curves in your line. When someone offers advice, look for theirs.

## 3. Does it span their set?

The right people can give the wrong advice. Not because they’re wrong, but because they’re answering a question they’ve never actually faced. You can ask a brilliant founder who’s experienced tremendous ups and downs about how to sell and strike gold. Ask them for relationship advice and your guess is as good as theirs. Everyone has a span, the set of situations their experience can reach. Any answer outside that set is extrapolation. Extrapolation sounds as confident as knowledge. Verify whether your problem lands within their span.

The world is noisy, and it’s easy to feel lost. Cutting down the clutter makes the next step far easier to take. I’d be lying if I said I’ve mastered this skill. That lost feeling comes back over and over, and it forces me to apply the filters not only to inbound advice, but to my own.
