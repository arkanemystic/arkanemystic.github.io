# The derivative of advice

There’s a quote I think about often from John Ternus: “Always assume you're as smart as anyone else in the room, but never assume that you know as much as they do.” I used to underestimate my capabilities. I’ve grown since then, and have the confidence to step into the fog of war, knowing I can work it out. But that hasn’t eroded the other half of the quote. Others know far more than I do, but how do I discriminate useful vs useless information? Everyone has a take. Listening to them all is a poor use of resources. These are the guidelines I’ve come to follow over time.

## 1. Velocity outweighs position

This wasn’t initially clear to me. I’d grown up chasing prestige. I looked up to those who pulled it off: good schools, titles, awards. I assumed everyone who earned them had something worth sharing. Some did. But it was a lagging indicator. Credentials measure position, where someone is right now. What matters is velocity, how fast they’re getting better at something real. More often than not, it’s those who focus on real problems, sharpen their skills towards a greater vision, that lead the world. The credentials, merits, and prestige follow. Don’t listen to someone purely because of their rank. Discern value from their ability to make hard turns. The decisions they made, the reasoning behind them, and the conviction that powered it all.

## 2. Inflection points matter

Inflection points occur when acceleration inverts. Linear growth gets you a steady path, but sharp inflections force you to compound wisdom. Wins and losses count towards that. The more inflection points in a set amount of time, the more they’ve experienced. Everyone knows how to walk a straight line; it’s when you’re thrown off track that your strengths shine. I’ve grown to become comfortable sharing my fair share of failures, because I realized it doesn’t convey weakness. It builds identity. You are who you are because of the curves in your line. When someone offers advice, look for theirs.

## 3. Does it span their set?

The right people can give the wrong advice. Not because they’re wrong, but because they’re answering a question they’ve never actually faced. You can ask a brilliant founder who’s experienced tremendous ups and downs about how to sell and strike gold. Ask them for relationship advice and your guess is as good as theirs. Everyone has a span, the set of situations their experience can reach. Any answer outside that set is extrapolation. Extrapolation sounds as confident as knowledge.

I learned this firsthand when I left Ohio State in January of 2026. I felt trapped and numb to my surroundings. I had friends I could depend on, classes I was genuinely curious about, but I lacked clarity in thought. I’d pay attention in lecture for math because the classes were interesting, but the moment I set foot in a gen ed, I couldn’t bring myself to pick up a pen. It was scary to leave. I didn’t have a set plan, and my friends and parents (rightfully) doubted my choices. I needed to leave that lethargic feeling. It was my choice, but I was ashamed of it. I was reluctant to share the news with others, afraid of their passing judgment. If there’s one thing I regret in that phase, it’s the fact that I made that choice with my head held low. I spent too much time speculating about what others thought, when in reality I was slowly building a future for myself I could not have envisioned otherwise. This was when I fell in love with formal methods, and the potential it held for AI safety and reasoning. That was an inflection point. I’m glad I took a step back from school; it gave me the clarity I was craving.

My friends and parents had their reasons to doubt, but they couldn’t relate to the problem I faced. It was outside their span. That being said, their advice did help me structure my time, maintain good physical and mental health, and push through times of doubt. Verify whether your problem lands within their span. If it doesn’t, decompose it into pieces that do.

## Closing

The world is noisy, and it’s easy to feel lost. Cutting down the clutter makes the next step far easier to take. I’d be lying if I said I’ve mastered this skill. That lost feeling comes back over and over, and it forces me to apply the filters not only to inbound advice, but to my own.
